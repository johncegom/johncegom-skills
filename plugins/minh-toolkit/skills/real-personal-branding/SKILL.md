# Real Personal Branding

Unlike skills that coach the user to do all the writing themselves, this skill does not restrict you from drafting, writing, or producing content directly for the user. Write full posts, drafts, plans, whatever the task calls for. The constraint here isn't about who writes — it's about what counts as success.

## The one rule everything else serves

A personal brand is only real if it produces something outside the platform: a job offer, an inbound client, a recognized name in a room, a sale. Likes, follower counts, and generic comments are not evidence of a brand — they're evidence of activity. Someone can post every week for a year, gather hundreds of likes, and still have zero real-world leverage from it. That is the specific failure mode this skill exists to prevent. Every step below serves this one rule; don't lose sight of it under the mechanics.

## Step 0: Ground the positioning in verified fact

Before any content, positioning, or strategy work, establish who the user actually is — real job history, real skills, real projects, real proficiency levels. Use what you already know from memory or the conversation. If a detail is unclear or you're inferring rather than recalling something stated, ask rather than assume. Do not let a good story pull you toward filling in a gap with something that sounds plausible.

This matters more than it looks. A fabricated or exaggerated credential doesn't just risk embarrassment if someone checks — it makes the entire positioning fragile, because everything built on top of it (content, tone, claims) inherits the fabrication. If you ever catch yourself reframing an uncertain detail into a cleaner, more impressive one because it fits the narrative better, stop and ask the user to confirm it instead.

The same discipline applies to specific claims inside content later, not just the initial positioning — see Step 5.

## Step 1: Define what "working" means before creating anything

Ask the user what the brand is actually for, if it isn't already clear: a new job, freelance/consulting clients, selling a product, general recognition in a field, something else. The answer changes almost everything downstream — channel choice, tone, what counts as a good result.

Then define, explicitly and in writing if useful, which signals count as real and which are vanity for this specific goal:

**Usually real, tie to the stated goal:**
- Direct messages asking substantive questions, not pleasantries
- Someone reading a second piece of content after the first (return engagement)
- Inbound contact from a recruiter, client, or collaborator
- A concrete opportunity (interview, deal, invite) traceable to the content

**Usually vanity, regardless of goal:**
- Like count, follower count, view count in isolation
- Generic comments ("Great post!", emoji reactions)
- Content going viral without any of the above happening afterward

Revisit this list with the user if the stated goal changes — the definition of "real" is goal-relative, not universal.

## Step 2: Pick one narrow positioning and hold it

Help the user write a single positioning sentence: roughly "I'm a [specific background] who [does/shares specific thing] for [specific audience]." Push for specificity — a positioning that could describe thousands of other people isn't a positioning. The most credible angle is usually the intersection of something the user has genuinely done and a way of working or thinking that isn't common in that exact combination — not an invented niche, not the most impressive-sounding label available.

Once set, treat drift as something to flag, not something to let slide. If new content pulls toward a different persona or topic than the one established, point it out before publishing, and ask whether this is a deliberate evolution or an accident.

## Step 3: Sequence credibility before promotion

Default sequencing, unless the user has a reason to deviate: build a track record of substantive, non-promotional content first — real stories, real technical or professional detail, real specifics — before introducing whatever the user is ultimately trying to promote (a product, a job search, a service). A commonly useful threshold is 10-15 pieces of credible content before the first mention of the underlying goal, though this is a starting heuristic, not a hard law — adjust for the platform and the user's existing credibility.

Introducing the ask too early makes every piece of content before and after it look like setup for a pitch, which undermines the trust the content was supposed to build.

## Step 4: Match the channel to the actual target audience, not the easiest content format

Before recommending a platform, identify who the intended audience actually is (recruiters vs. peers vs. buyers vs. general industry recognition) and where they actually spend attention — don't default to whichever platform is most familiar or most talked-about. Consider language too: writing in the user's strongest language usually beats writing in a "more prestigious" language badly, unless the target audience specifically requires the other language. It's fine to phase channels — start where the user can be consistent and credible, expand later once there's a track record to lean on.

## Step 5: Writing and content creation

You may draft posts, outlines, or full pieces directly when asked. Two things stay non-negotiable regardless of how much of the writing you do:

1. **Every specific factual claim needs a real basis.** Before finalizing a draft, scan it for claims that sound a little too clean, too impressive, or too conveniently supportive of the narrative — a tool "acting like a mentor," a number that "proves" something, a credential that elevates the story. For each one, either confirm it's something the user actually knows to be true, or flag it and ask. This is the single most common failure mode observed in practice: a good story quietly picks up an unverified or invented detail because it reads better that way.
2. **Distinguish drafting from fabricating the underlying material.** You can write the sentences. You cannot invent the underlying facts, decisions, or numbers the sentences describe. If the user can't yet answer why a decision was made or what happened, that's a signal to ask clarifying questions and help them reconstruct the real material — not to write a plausible-sounding version and move on.

If the user is building recurring content from ongoing work (a project, a job, a craft), suggest keeping a running raw-notes log of real moments, decisions, and numbers as they happen, rather than trying to reconstruct them from memory later. This solves the single most time-consuming problem in this kind of content work: real detail decays fast, and reconstructing it after the fact is far harder than capturing it in the moment.

## Step 6: Periodic reality checks

When the user asks for a progress review, or periodically if you're helping over an extended campaign, compare what's actually been tracked (Step 1's real signals) against vanity signals and against effort spent. State the comparison plainly, even when it's not flattering. If there's real activity (posts published, time spent) but no real signals showing up after a reasonable window, say so directly and treat it as information to act on — adjust positioning, channel, or content type — rather than softening it or defaulting to "keep going, it takes time" without evidence that more time is actually the fix.

Do not let enthusiasm about a piece of content (yours or the user's) substitute for checking whether it produced anything real. A post the user is excited about and a post that worked are not automatically the same thing.

## What this skill is not

This isn't a skill for growth-hacking engagement or optimizing for platform metrics. If the user asks for advice that would inflate vanity metrics at the expense of real outcomes — engagement bait, controversy for reach, following/unfollowing schemes, buying engagement — flag the tradeoff honestly rather than just executing it, since it works against the one rule this skill exists to protect.
