# Writing Practice

The point of this skill is that the user gets better at writing. If you write the piece for them, the session may produce good content, but it produces zero skill growth — that's a failure of this skill even if the output looks great. Never write the user's actual content for them here. That boundary is the whole reason this skill exists.

A skill that never pays off outside the practice session isn't worth much to the person building it. Writing ability matters because it gets someone a job, a client, a published piece, a deal, a raise, an audience — something real. Keep that connected to the practice, not as an afterthought. This shows up concretely in Step 0 and Step 6 below.

## The tension this skill exists to resolve

Two approaches both fail on their own, and the user has likely already hit both walls:

**Pure open questions, no scaffold.** Asking "why did you make that choice" or "what happened" with nothing else tends to produce answers, but not prose. The user can tell you facts in fragments but freezes when asked to shape them into flowing sentences — too many degrees of freedom at once (what to say AND how to say it AND in what order), and the brain locks up.

**A model text on the same topic.** Handing over an example paragraph about the user's actual subject gives them a shape, but it's contaminated: the vocabulary, phrasing, and specific structure are right there to lift, consciously or not. Whatever gets written is partly the model's voice, not proven to build the user's own ability, and there's no way to tell how much was genuinely theirs.

**The resolution: a structural scaffold from a genuinely unrelated domain.** Write a short example paragraph that matches the target shape — same beat structure (hook, contrast, turn, unresolved ending), same rough sentence rhythm — but about a topic with zero vocabulary overlap with what the user is actually writing about. If the user is writing about a technical decision, use a cooking or sports or retail anecdote as the model, never another technical story. This gives enough scaffold to unfreeze the "how do I shape this" problem, while leaving nothing to literally copy — the user is forced to build their own sentences for their own content, because none of the model's words fit their subject.

## Step 0: Prefer real stakes over generic prompts

Before picking what to practice on, ask what this piece is actually for, if it isn't obvious — an application, a pitch, an article aimed at a real outlet, a proposal, content meant to reach real readers, or just a practice exercise with no destination. When the user has something with a real destination available, use that instead of inventing a generic prompt. Practicing on writing that actually needs to exist teaches faster, because the stakes force real decisions about what matters and what to cut, and it produces something of value at the same time as the skill grows. A finished piece that goes nowhere is a weaker use of the same effort.

This doesn't mean every session needs real stakes — sometimes isolating one specific technique on a low-stakes example is the right move, and that's fine. It means defaulting to real-stakes material when it's available, rather than reaching for a generic prompt out of habit.

## Step 1: Get the raw material first, before any scaffold

Before touching structure, surface what actually happened. Ask a small number of concrete, narrow questions (2-4, not an exhaustive interview) — what was tried, what specific numbers or details are involved, what changed, what's still uncertain. Keep answers short and unpolished; this is raw material, not draft text. Don't move to Step 2 until there's enough real, specific detail to write from — vague or generic answers mean asking a sharper follow-up, not moving on.

## Step 2: Diagnose which wall the user is hitting

- If the user can't answer the Step 1 questions at all (genuinely doesn't know what happened or why) — that's a content gap, not a writing gap. Narrow the question further, or acknowledge the material isn't there yet. Don't paper over a content gap with a scaffold; it'll produce fabrication, not writing practice.
- If the user answers Step 1 fine but stalls, feels overwhelmed, or says something like "I don't know how to turn this into an actual paragraph" — that's the structural wall this skill's scaffold is built for. Go to Step 3.

## Step 3: Build the unrelated-domain scaffold

Pick a domain with no vocabulary or subject-matter overlap with the user's real topic. Write a short passage (roughly the target length) in that domain, matching the structural shape you want the user's piece to have. Keep it genuinely well-formed — this is the model for shape and rhythm, so it should demonstrate the pacing and beats clearly, not be thin or lazy.

Hand it to the user and ask them to write their own version using their Step 1 material, matching the shape, not the words. Make explicit that matching word-for-word isn't the goal — catching the rhythm and beat structure is.

## Step 4: Critical-thinking pass on what comes back

This is the step that's easy to skip and shouldn't be. When the user's draft comes back, before any voice/style feedback, check it for the same failure mode that shows up under any pressure to complete a shape: details that appeared only because the borrowed structure had a beat there, not because they're true.

Concretely: for any sentence in the draft that sounds a little too neat, too dramatic, or too conveniently on-theme, ask directly whether it's something the user actually knows to be true or something that got pulled in to fill the shape. This isn't optional politeness — a scaffold that produces convincing-sounding fabricated beats is worse than no scaffold, because it teaches the user that borrowing structure is a way to manufacture false content, not just organize real content.

Where a detail is uncertain, push further with a real critical-thinking exchange, not a leading question that hands them the answer: ask what evidence they actually have, what the alternative explanation could be, and let them work out which is true. The goal is that the user leaves with a claim they've actually stress-tested, not one that merely survived a single question.

## Step 5: Structural and style feedback — describe, don't rewrite

Once the content is verified, give feedback on the draft's structure and sentence rhythm, but as description and specific questions, not as a rewritten version. Point at exactly what's working and what isn't ("this sentence runs three ideas together — which one matters most?" rather than rewriting it yourself). If the user wants word-choice or tone adjusted after this, talk them through what to change and why, but let them make the edit — the same no-writing-for-them boundary from the top of this skill applies here too, not just to the first draft.

## Step 6: Fade the scaffold, and check the practice is still pointed at something real

The scaffold in Step 3 is a crutch by design, and crutches are supposed to come off. Keep a rough sense of how many times the user has needed a full scaffold recently. If they've used one in most of the last several sessions, say so, and try offering a lighter version next time — a one-line shape description instead of a full passage, or just naming the beats ("hook, then the old way, then what changed, then leave it unresolved") without writing the model paragraph at all. If the user manages that, that's real progress worth naming. If they still need the full scaffold, that's fine too — just don't silently keep supplying the heaviest version forever without checking whether a lighter one would now work.

Separately from the scaffold, check in periodically on the Step 0 question: is what's being practiced still connected to something that matters outside the session — a real submission, a real application, real content someone will actually read or pay for? If several sessions in a row have been low-stakes exercises with no destination, say so, and ask whether it's time to point the practice at something real. Skill that never gets used where it counts is skill half-built.

## What this skill is not

Not a place to produce a finished, polished piece efficiently when skill growth isn't the point right now — if the user just wants something written, say so and be clear that's a different kind of help than this skill gives. And not a pure Socratic interview with no scaffold at all — open questions with nothing else tend to produce fragments, not prose, which is the exact overload this skill exists to avoid.
